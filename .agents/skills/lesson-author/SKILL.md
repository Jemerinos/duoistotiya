# Lesson author

## Goal
Produce a clear, engaging, evidence-aware lesson that teaches rather than merely lists facts. Work only on the requested lessons and use the current runtime content as the starting point.

## Procedure
1. Identify the lesson's exact topic, period, geography, learning goal, current reading, questions, glossary entries, people layer, and existing source links. Inspect the relevant curriculum and detail files; do not create a disconnected enrichment file.
2. Build a short factual outline before writing: background, chronology, key actors and interests, mechanisms/turning points, immediate consequences, longer-term effects, and evidence/uncertainty.
3. Research claims using reliable sources. Prefer primary sources for evidence and reputable academic, museum, archive, university, or educational sources for context. Cross-check dates, names, figures, and contested interpretations. Never invent a source or imply a source supports a claim you did not verify.
4. Write natural Russian for an interested school-age reader. Explain unfamiliar terms at first use. Use concrete examples and transitions so the text reads as one coherent account, not a checklist.
5. For a typical event lesson, aim around 500–800 words when justified by scope. Short methods, overview, or comparison lessons may be shorter; do not add padding.
6. Explain several distinct causes when historically appropriate, separating long-term conditions, immediate triggers, decisions by actors, and structural factors. Explain consequences at different time scales and avoid treating correlation as proof of causation.
7. Represent historical disagreement fairly. Label interpretations as interpretations, note uncertainty, and avoid present-day assumptions being projected onto historical actors without evidence.
8. Check consistency with the lesson title, timeline, glossary, people, images, quizzes, and answer keys. Do not add facts that make existing questions incorrect.
9. Keep citations or source links near the relevant reading. Prefer a small set of high-quality sources over a long uncurated link list.
10. Report what was added, sources actually checked, and unresolved uncertainty.

## Quality checklist
- [ ] The opening explains where and when the reader is, and why the topic matters.
- [ ] The narrative has a clear chronology and named actors.
- [ ] Terms are explained in context, not just listed.
- [ ] Causes are separated and explained; consequences are concrete.
- [ ] Claims are proportionate to the evidence; uncertainty is explicit.
- [ ] The Russian is fluent, specific, and free of generic filler.
- [ ] Sources exist and support the claims attributed to them.
- [ ] The reading aligns with lesson activities, visuals, and the rest of the curriculum.
- [ ] Runtime content is verified, not just source-file content.
